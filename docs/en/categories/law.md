# Law / Legal Studies

Use this guide for law, legal studies, public policy law, business law, criminal law, constitutional law, torts, contracts, administrative law, international law, and jurisprudence.

## Review goal

Convert legal materials into an exam-ready framework of rules, elements, authorities, policy rationales, issue-spotting triggers, and structured application to facts.

## What to extract from materials

- Legal doctrines, statutory provisions, case holdings, tests, elements, exceptions, and burdens of proof.
- Fact patterns used by the instructor, hypotheticals, tutorial questions, and past-paper issues.
- Competing interpretations, policy arguments, dissenting reasoning, and reform debates.
- Jurisdiction-specific requirements and citation expectations.
- Marking rubrics, model answers, and feedback comments.

## Chapter note structure

1. **Topic map**: legal area, sub-issues, relationship among doctrines.
2. **Rule statements**: concise rule, source, elements, exceptions.
3. **Case table**: case name, material facts, issue, holding, reasoning, exam use.
4. **Statutory interpretation notes**: key wording, threshold, defenses, remedies.
5. **Issue triggers**: factual clues that activate each doctrine.
6. **Application patterns**: how to argue both sides.
7. **Policy/rationale**: why the rule exists and criticism.
8. **Common pitfalls**: conclusion-first answers, missing elements, unsupported assertions.
9. **Practice hypotheticals** with model IRAC answers.

## Typical exam question types

- Problem questions / issue-spotting hypotheticals.
- Case analysis and rule explanation.
- Statutory interpretation.
- Essay questions on doctrine, policy, reform, or theory.
- Compare jurisdictions, cases, principles, or tests.
- Remedy, liability, defense, or procedural questions.

## IRAC / CRAC template

Use **Issue → Rule → Application → Conclusion** or **Conclusion → Rule → Application → Conclusion**.

For each issue:

1. State the legal issue as a question.
2. State the rule with elements and authority.
3. Apply each element to specific facts.
4. Address counterarguments and uncertain facts.
5. Reach a qualified conclusion.
6. Mention remedy, defense, or consequence when relevant.

## Essay template

Use: **Thesis → Legal framework → Authorities → Analysis → Counterargument → Evaluation → Conclusion**.

A strong legal essay should not merely describe rules; it should evaluate doctrine, policy, coherence, fairness, efficiency, rights, institutional competence, and reform implications.

## Question bank rules

Generate:

- 20% rule/element recall and case significance.
- 40% issue-spotting hypotheticals.
- 20% short case/statute application questions.
- 20% essay or policy evaluation questions.

Model answers should show facts-to-law reasoning and include alternative arguments where the facts are ambiguous.

## High-yield exam prediction signals

Prioritize topics that:

- Have multi-element tests and exceptions.
- Have landmark cases or statutory provisions.
- Are repeatedly used in hypotheticals or tutorials.
- Allow plausible arguments on both sides.
- Connect doctrine to policy debates or remedies.

## Active study plan

- Memorize rule skeletons, not paragraphs.
- Practice issue spotting from short fact patterns daily.
- Build case cards: facts, issue, rule, reasoning, exam use.
- Rehearse timed IRAC answers and compare against rubrics.
- Create contrast tables for similar doctrines and defenses.

## Scoring rubric

Award marks for issue identification, accurate rule and authority, element-by-element application, use of facts, counterarguments, structured conclusion, and policy insight where relevant.
