# Humanities

Use this guide for history, philosophy, literature, languages, classics, religious studies, cultural studies, linguistics, art history, and related humanities courses.

## Review goal

Help the student build interpretive depth: key texts, concepts, contexts, arguments, evidence, historiography, close reading, comparative analysis, and essay-ready thesis structures.

## What to extract from materials

- Primary texts, passages, artworks, historical events, thinkers, theories, schools, and debates.
- Keywords, concepts, motifs, themes, chronology, genre conventions, and cultural context.
- Instructor interpretations, lecture arguments, tutorial debates, and essay prompts.
- Evidence types: quotations, episodes, images, archival facts, linguistic examples, or philosophical claims.
- Assessment criteria for essays, commentaries, translations, or short-answer exams.

## Chapter note structure

1. **Central question**: what interpretive or historical problem the chapter addresses.
2. **Context**: period, author, movement, school, social/political background.
3. **Key concepts and terms**: definition, nuance, example.
4. **Argument map**: main claim, subclaims, evidence, implications.
5. **Primary evidence bank**: quotations/events/passages/images with analysis.
6. **Debate or historiography**: competing interpretations.
7. **Comparison table**: thinkers, texts, periods, schools, or themes.
8. **Essay thesis templates**.
9. **Common pitfalls**: plot summary, unsupported claims, anachronism, vague comparison.
10. **Practice prompts** with outlines.

## Typical exam question types

- Define and explain concepts, movements, periods, or methods.
- Close reading or passage commentary.
- Compare texts, authors, thinkers, events, or interpretations.
- Thematic essay or argumentative essay.
- Chronology, cause-consequence, continuity-change questions.
- Translation, grammar, or textual analysis for language courses.

## Analysis templates

### Textual / close reading

Use: **Observation → Evidence → Technique/Form → Meaning → Context → Argument significance**.

### History question

Use: **Claim → Chronology/context → Causes → Evidence → Consequences → Historiographical debate → Judgment**.

### Philosophy question

Use: **Thesis → Argument reconstruction → Assumptions → Objection → Reply → Evaluation**.

## Question bank rules

Generate:

- 20% key term and context checks.
- 25% evidence-based short answers.
- 25% comparison and interpretation questions.
- 20% essay prompts with outlines.
- 10% passage/image/source commentary.

Answers should model how to move from evidence to interpretation, not just state facts.

## High-yield exam prediction signals

Prioritize topics that:

- Anchor multiple lectures or readings.
- Are linked to named theories, debates, or turning points.
- Have strong evidence banks: quotations, dates, images, passages.
- Fit common essay verbs: compare, evaluate, discuss, analyze, explain.

## Active study plan

- Use active recall by writing thesis statements from memory.
- Build evidence cards with quotation/event + analytical use.
- Practice one-paragraph arguments under time pressure.
- Create timelines, concept maps, and comparison grids.
- Teach a debate aloud: one interpretation, counterinterpretation, judgment.

## Scoring rubric

Award marks for thesis clarity, contextual accuracy, evidence selection, depth of analysis, engagement with debate, structure, and precise expression.
