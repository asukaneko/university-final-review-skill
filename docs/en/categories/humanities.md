# Humanities

Applies to history, philosophy, literature, languages, classics, religious studies, cultural studies, linguistics, art history, and other humanities courses.

## Sub-discipline lookup

| Sub-discipline | Typical courses | Key question types |
| --- | --- | --- |
| History | Chinese History, World History, Historiography, Specialized History | Timeline analysis, cause-effect, historiographical debates |
| Philosophy | Chinese Philosophy, Western Philosophy, Ethics, Aesthetics, Logic | Argument reconstruction, concept analysis, comparative evaluation |
| Literature | Chinese Lit, World Lit, Comparative Lit, Literary Theory | Close reading, thematic analysis, comparative study |
| Linguistics | Intro Linguistics, Modern Chinese, Classical Chinese, Applied Ling | Structural analysis, corpus interpretation, theory application |
| Religious Studies | Intro to Religion, Buddhism, Christianity, Islam | Concept explanation, comparative study, cultural analysis |
| Art History | Chinese Art History, Western Art History, Design History | Work analysis, style comparison, historical context |

## Review goal

Develop interpretive depth: key texts, concepts, contexts, arguments, evidence, historiographical debates, close reading ability, comparative analysis, and essay-ready thesis structures.

## What to extract from materials

- Primary sources, passages, artworks, historical events, thinkers, theories, schools, debates.
- Keywords, concepts, motifs, themes, timelines, genre rules, cultural contexts.
- Instructor interpretations, classroom arguments, seminar debates, essay prompts.
- Evidence types: quotations, events, images, archival facts, linguistic examples, philosophical propositions.
- Essay, text annotation, translation, or short-answer scoring criteria.

## Chapter note structure

1. **Central question**: What interpretive or historical problem does this chapter address.
2. **Context**: Period, author, movement, school, social/political context.
3. **Key concepts and terms**: Definitions, nuances, examples.
4. **Argument map**: Claims, sub-claims, evidence, implications.
5. **Evidence bank**: Quotes/passes/images and their analytical uses.
6. **Debates or historiographical perspectives**: Different interpretations.
7. **Comparison table**: Thinkers, texts, periods, schools, or themes.
8. **Essay thesis template**.
9. **Common errors**: Plot summary, unsupported claims, anachronism, vague comparison.
10. **Practice questions** and outlines.

## Common question types

- Define and explain concepts, movements, periods, or methods.
- Close reading or passage annotation.
- Compare texts, authors, thinkers, events, or interpretations.
- Thematic or argumentative essay.
- Timeline, cause-effect, continuity-change.
- Translation, grammar, or textual analysis in language courses.

## Problem-solving templates

### Close reading

Use: **Observation → Evidence → Technique/Form → Meaning → Context → Relevance to thesis**.

1. Observation: What feature does this passage/image have.
2. Evidence: What specific details support your observation.
3. Technique/Form: What手法/strategy the author/artist uses.
4. Meaning: What effect these techniques produce.
5. Context: In what historical/cultural/authorial context to understand.
6. Relevance to thesis: How it serves the overall argument.

### History question

Use: **Thesis → Timeline/Context → Causes → Evidence → Consequences → Historiographical debate → Judgment**.

1. Thesis: What is your core argument.
2. Timeline/Context: When and in what environment events occurred.
3. Causes: Proximate and deep causes.
4. Evidence: What historical sources support.
5. Consequences: Short-term and long-term impacts.
6. Historiographical debate: Different historians' interpretations.
7. Judgment: Which interpretation you support and why.

### Philosophy question

Use: **Thesis → Argument reconstruction → Assumptions → Rebuttals → Responses → Evaluation**.

1. Thesis: The thinker's core claim.
2. Argument reconstruction: Restate the argument in clear logic.
3. Assumptions: What premises the argument relies on.
4. Rebuttals: What objections exist.
5. Responses: How the thinker responds to objections.
6. Evaluation: Whether you find the argument convincing.

### Comparison question

Use: **Comparison dimensions → Analysis of each → Commonalities → Differences → Explanation of differences → Evaluation**.

1. Determine comparison dimensions (themes, methods, impact, context, etc.).
2. Analyze each subject across all dimensions.
3. Identify commonalities and differences.
4. Explain reasons for differences.
5. Offer evaluative conclusion.

### Argumentative essay

1. Determine a contestable, evidence-supported thesis.
2. List sub-claims (each with evidence).
3. Organize structure (progressive, parallel, or contrastive).
4. Address counterarguments.
5. Draw a nuanced conclusion.

## Question bank rules

Suggested ratio:

- 20% keyword and context checks.
- 25% evidence-based short answers.
- 25% comparison and interpretation.
- 20% essay prompts and outlines.
- 10% passage/image/source annotation.

Answers should demonstrate moving from evidence to interpretation, not just listing facts.

## High-yield exam point signals

Prioritize:

- Core themes running across multiple sessions or readings.
- Content linked to named theories, debates, or turning points.
- Content with rich evidence banks: quotes, dates, images, passages.
- Content suited to "compare, evaluate, discuss, analyze, explain" essay verbs.

## Active review methods

- Active recall: write thesis statements and key arguments from memory.
- Create evidence cards: quotes/events + analytical angles they support.
- Timed paragraph writing (complete one argument in 10 minutes).
- Make timelines, concept maps, and comparison tables.
- Verbalize a debate: one interpretation → counter-interpretation → your judgment.
- Imitate the argument structure of model answers.

## Common error-prone points

| Error type | Example | Prevention |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plot summary | Only retelling the story | Include analysis in every paragraph |
| Unsupported claims | "This poem is beautiful" | Cite specific lines |
| Anachronism | Applying modern ideas to historical figures | Note historical context |
| Vague comparison | "Both are important" | Find specific commonalities and differences |
| Unclear thesis | "This topic is complex" | Write a specific, contestable thesis |
| Ignoring debate | Only presenting one view | Mention at least two perspectives |

## Scoring rubric

Points for: thesis clarity, contextual accuracy, evidence selection, analytical depth, engagement with debate, structure, and linguistic precision.
